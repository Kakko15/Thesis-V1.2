Comparative Performance Analysis

Does retrieval beat the model's own memory?

The short version

We asked the same 40 questions twice. Once to Gemini on its own, working purely from what it already knew. Once to the RAG system, which may only use text it retrieves from the CCSICT thesis archive. A panel of three faculty wrote the correct answer to every question in advance, and both sets of answers were graded against those.

Across all 40, retrieval scored higher — 0.302 against 0.217 — and the gap is statistically real, not chance (p = 0.00003). Retrieval won on 29 of the 40 questions.

But read this before quoting that number important

Most of that win comes from questions the archive cannot answer, where the RAG system correctly says so and the plain model invents something. That is a real and valuable behaviour — but it is not "accuracy". On the 16 questions the archive genuinely covers, retrieval is ahead by a smaller margin that the statistics cannot confirm is real at this sample size. Section 3.2.5 of the paper quotes that smaller figure, so it is the honest headline.

Where the gain actually comes from

Each question was labelled in advance with whether the released corpus could answer it at all. Each row below is one of those groups. The dot is how much better RAG did; the bar through it is the range the true value plausibly sits in.

How to read this chart

The vertical line at zero means "no difference". A dot to the right of it means retrieval did better; to the left, the plain model did better.

The horizontal bar is a 95% confidence interval — the range the real difference probably falls in. If that bar crosses the zero line, the result could still be zero, so we cannot claim a difference. That is exactly what happens on the present row.

  • Mean difference (RAG − baseline)
  • 95% confidence interval

Three groups clear zero; the one that matters does not. The present interval runs from −0.019 to +0.139, so at 16 questions the gain on covered material is positive in direction but not established. The two smallest groups (4 and 3 questions) reach significance on far too few pairs to carry a claim by themselves.

Why the answerable questions underperform

Eight of the sixteen covered questions returned a "could not verify" notice instead of an answer. Retrieval was not at fault — all eight pulled the full five passages at similarity 0.67–0.74, far above the 0.30 threshold. The answers were discarded afterwards, at citation checking.

  • Real answers (32)
  • Notices (8)

Faithfulness splits 0.78 against 0.20 along that line, which is what makes the mechanism measurable rather than a guess. Context Precision is low in both groups and is a separate, still-open question about retrieval ranking.

What actually happened, from the run log

Five of the eight logged Citation repair remained invalid — both the model's own repair attempt and the deterministic enforce_citation_coverage pass left a substantive sentence without a citation. The other three logged Citation repair failed (TruncatedGeneration), where the repair call itself ran into the 2,000-token output ceiling that applies when no gateway is configured.

This is deliberate design, not a bug: the pipeline throws away an answer it cannot cite rather than stapling citation markers onto uncited claims. What this run adds is the price of that choice on exactly the questions the archive can answer — and it is the largest single lever on the figure the paper quotes.
